# Spoken Slovak, Register, Regional Variation, and Social Formulas

Use this to write natural dialogue, choose a register, label colloquial or dialect words, address people correctly, and handle greetings, wishes, idioms, and cultural occasions. Default output is standard Slovak; produce colloquial or regional forms only when the task calls for authentic speech.

## 1. Register labels

Slovak dictionaries (KSSJ, SSSJ) mark words with labels. Map them to this site's dictionary fields (`register`, `dialect`):

| Label     | Meaning                        | Example                   | Site field               |
| --------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| (none)    | neutral standard               | `peniaze`, `pracovať`     | —                        |
| `kniž.`   | bookish, formal written        | `nuž`, `avšak`            | —                        |
| `hovor.`  | colloquial, accepted in speech | `fotka`, `mobil`, `kamoš` | `register: "colloquial"` |
| `expr.`   | expressive, emotional          | `chlapisko`, `psisko`     | usually `colloquial`     |
| `slang.`  | slang                          | `prachy`, `makať`         | `register: "slang"`      |
| `subšt.`  | substandard                    | `furt`, `jak`             | `colloquial` or `slang`  |
| `nár.`    | dialectal                      | `grule`                   | `dialect: true`          |
| `zastar.` | archaic                        | —                         | —                        |
| `odb.`    | technical                      | `substantívum`            | —                        |

## 2. Features of everyday spoken Slovak

Use these in informal dialogue; keep them out of neutral or formal text.

| Feature                             | Spoken                                                   | Standard written           |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| relative `čo` for people and things | `ten chlap, čo tam stojí`                                | `ten muž, ktorý tam stojí` |
| `šak` for `však`                    | `Šak som ti to hovoril.`                                 | `Veď som ti to hovoril.`   |
| shortened 1pl                       | `môžme`                                                  | `môžeme`                   |
| `furt`                              | `Furt prší.`                                             | `Stále prší.`              |
| fillers                             | `no`, `tak`, `akože`, `proste`, `vlastne`                | —                          |
| informal yes                        | `hej`, `jasné`, `jasnačka`, `no`                         | `áno`                      |
| reactions                           | `fakt?`, `vážne?`, `super`, `fajn`, `v pohode`, `nevadí` | —                          |

Common colloquial vocabulary: `kamoš/kamoška` (friend), `chalan` (lad), `baba` (girl, woman; can be rude), `frajer/frajerka` (boyfriend/girlfriend; also show-off), `fotka`, `mobil`, `telka` (TV), `matika` (maths), `intrák` (dorm), `pokecať si` (have a chat), `makať` (work hard, slang), `prachy` (money, slang), `nemám šajn` (no idea).

### Informal greetings and goodbyes

`Ahoj` (hi/bye), `Čau` (hi/bye), `Servus` (hi, older/regional), `Zdravím` (semi-formal hello, common in emails), `Maj sa (pekne)` / `Majte sa`, `Ozvi sa` (get in touch), `Dovi` (bye, colloquial), `Pá` (bye, to children or very close people).

## 3. Names, address, and titles

### First names and their familiar forms

| Name        | Familiar      | Name       | Familiar |
| ----------- | ------------- | ---------- | -------- |
| `Ján`       | `Janko`       | `Zuzana`   | `Zuzka`  |
| `Peter`     | `Peťo`        | `Katarína` | `Katka`  |
| `Pavol`     | `Paľo`        | `Mária`    | `Majka`  |
| `Jozef`     | `Jožo, Jožko` | `Jana`     | `Janka`  |
| `Juraj`     | `Ďuro, Ďurko` | `Eva`      | `Evka`   |
| `Michal`    | `Mišo, Miško` | `Daniela`  | `Danka`  |
| `Martin`    | `Maťo`        | `Ivana`    | `Ivka`   |
| `František` | `Fero`        | `Ladislav` | `Laco`   |

Family address forms used as vocatives: `mami`, `oci`, `babi`, `dedko`. Familiar forms imply `ty`.

### Formal address

- `pán` + surname, `pani` + surname: `pán Novák`, `pani Nováková`. Stay in the nominative when addressing: `Dobrý deň, pán Novák.`
- `pani` + first name with `vy` is common between colleagues of different ages: `Pani Jana, máte chvíľu?`
- Titles and functions are used in address: `pán doktor`, `pani doktorka`, `pán inžinier`, `pán profesor`, `pani profesorka`, `pán riaditeľ`, `pani riaditeľka`. Written titles: [orthography-and-punctuation.md](orthography-and-punctuation.md) §9.
- Feminine job titles are normal and expected for women: `učiteľka`, `lekárka`, `riaditeľka`, `ministerka`, `predsedníčka`.

### `ty` or `vy`

Adults who do not know each other use `vy`. Children, close friends, family, and young people among themselves use `ty`. Switching to `ty` is offered, typically by the older or more senior person (or the woman, traditionally): `Môžeme si tykať?` `Tykajme si.` Many workplaces and online communities default to `ty`; follow the context. Never mix: a text that opens with `vy` keeps `vy` forms throughout (pronouns, verbs, imperatives, possessives).

## 4. Social formulas

| Situation             | Formula                                                                                               |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| greeting by time      | `Dobré ráno` (morning), `Dobrý deň` (daytime, safe default), `Dobrý večer` (evening)                  |
| goodbye               | `Dovidenia`, `Do skorého videnia`, `Pekný deň/večer`, `Dobrú noc` (only when parting at night)        |
| thanks and reply      | `Ďakujem (pekne / veľmi pekne)`, `Vďaka` (informal) → `Niet za čo`, `Prosím`, `Rado sa stalo`         |
| apology and reply     | `Prepáč(te)`, `Ospravedlňujem sa` (formal), `Pardon` → `To je v poriadku`, `Nič sa nestalo`, `Nevadí` |
| entering a small shop | `Dobrý deň` to the staff; leaving: `Dovidenia`                                                        |
| ordering              | `Prosím si…`, `Dám si…`, `Ešte niečo?` – `Nie, to je všetko.` `Spolu alebo zvlášť?`                   |
| paying                | `Zaplatím.` `Účet, prosím.` `Môžem platiť kartou?` `Drobné si nechajte.`                              |
| on the phone          | answering `Prosím?` or `Haló`; `Tu je Jana Kováčová.`; `Môžem hovoriť s…?`                            |
| meals and drinks      | `Dobrú chuť!` (reply `Aj tebe / Aj vám`), `Na zdravie!` (toast)                                       |
| birthday, name day    | `Všetko najlepšie k narodeninám / k meninám!` `Veľa šťastia, zdravia!`                                |
| congratulations       | `Gratulujem!` `Blahoželám!` (more formal)                                                             |
| good luck             | `Veľa šťastia!` `Držím ti palce!` `Zlom väz!` (informal)                                              |
| travel                | `Šťastnú cestu!`                                                                                      |
| illness               | `Prajem skoré uzdravenie.` `Drž sa!`                                                                  |
| condolence            | `Úprimnú sústrasť.`                                                                                   |
| holidays              | `Veselé Vianoce!` `Šťastný nový rok!` `Príjemné prežitie sviatkov.`                                   |

## 5. Calendar and customs

Stable cultural facts are fine to teach. Verify any statement about public holidays or days off against current official sources; the legal status of some dates has changed.

- **Meniny (name days)**: every calendar day has one or more names; people are congratulated on their name day much like a birthday.
- **Vianoce**: `Štedrý deň` (24 December) is the main evening, with `kapustnica` (sauerkraut soup), `kapor` (carp), `oblátky` (wafers) with honey. `Mikuláš` (6 December) brings small gifts. `Silvester` (31 December).
- **Veľká noc** (Easter): on Easter Monday, `šibačka` and `oblievačka` (playful whipping with willow switches and splashing with water).
- **Fašiangy**: carnival season before Lent.
- **National commemorative dates**: 1 January (`Deň vzniku Slovenskej republiky`, 1993), 5 July (`sviatok svätého Cyrila a svätého Metoda`), 29 August (anniversary of the Slovak National Uprising, `SNP`, 1944), 1 September (`Deň Ústavy Slovenskej republiky`), 17 November (`Deň boja za slobodu a demokraciu`, 1989).
- **School grades** run 1 (best) to 5 (fail).

## 6. Regional variation

Standard Slovak is based mainly on central Slovak dialects. Three dialect groups:

| Group   | Area (examples)                      | Features you may meet                                                                          |
| ------- | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| western | Záhorie, Trnava, Nitra, Trenčín      | no `ä` (`maso`), fewer diphthongs; closer to Moravian Czech in the far west                    |
| central | Liptov, Orava, Turiec, Zvolen, Gemer | `ä`, `ô`, `ia/ie`, rhythmic law: the basis of the standard                                     |
| eastern | Spiš, Šariš, Zemplín, Abov           | stress on the penultimate syllable; no vowel length; `dz`, `c` for `ď`, `ť` (`dzeci` = `deti`) |

Regional vocabulary appears in speech and literature: potatoes are `zemiaky` in the standard, but `krumple`, `grule`, or `bandurky` depending on the region. Mark such entries `dialect: true` and keep standard Slovak in teaching examples unless the lesson is about variation.

Slovak speakers understand Czech, and Czech media are common in Slovakia, but Czech words in a Slovak text are errors, not variation. See [lexical-choice-and-interference.md](lexical-choice-and-interference.md) §6.
